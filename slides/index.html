Paw Paw

An iPhone and iPad game made for cats

Hi! Plan: how I designed it, a live demo, then the one big technical choice: SpriteKit.
The idea

One app, two players

  • The cat plays: critters run across the screen, a paw catches them.
  • The owner sets things up: round length, playground, cat profiles, stats.
  • Neither of them can do the other's job, and that drove most of the design.
The cat can't read or press Back. The owner isn't always watching. Every screen had to answer: who is holding this?

Design thinking

Onboarding: learn by doing

The owner will hand the device to a cat, so they need to know how it works before that.

1

Catch

Try catching real critters on a small live field.

2

Countdown

Lay the device flat, 3‑2‑1, let the cat go.

3

Hold to leave

Practise the hold. A tap only shows a hint.

4

Your cat

Pick a face and a name. The title greets the cat as you type.

Every step is optional and you can skip the whole thing, but trying it once makes the hold-to-leave obvious later. Faint critters drift behind the card, so it already feels like the game.
Design thinking

How does a cat say "again"?

The problem: a timed round ends, the cat is still hunting, and the owner may not be watching.

  • One critter, the lure, keeps roaming around the results card.
  • It hides under the card and peeks out from the edges.
  • Catching it starts the next round.
  • The owner picks: Keep going, 3 rounds or Stop.
Ignores taps for 1 s, so a late pounce doesn't count
Wanders off after 45 s without a touch
Owners hold Play Again on the card
The hunt never ends on a miss: in "3 rounds" and "Stop", the last lure is slower and never hides, so the cat always finishes on a catch. Lure catches don't score.
Design thinking

Moving like real prey

Dart and freeze

Each critter has its own style. Mice dart, crickets hop, feathers drift.

Twitch while still

Small twitches during a freeze keep the cat's eyes on it.

Hide and peek

Boxes and leaf piles hide critters, so the cat waits and pounces.

Heard first

Critters enter from off screen, with a sound from that side.

Also: at most two critters at once, dark backgrounds with colours cats see well, and a missed tap scares the nearest critter away. Speed adapts to how well the cat is doing.
Design thinking

One layout per screen shape

The rule is based on the space you actually have, not the device name: wide and tall enough means side by side. On the Duo it re-lays out mid-fold, because the size and the size class arrive in separate steps.
Accessibility, from class

Whose settings apply?

The menus follow the owner's settings. The game is for the cat, so it only changes enough for the cat to keep playing and the owner to still get out.

VoiceOver

Every hold gesture is also a simple action: "Leave game", "Play again", "Delete cat", "Reset stats".

Direct touch

With VoiceOver on, the play field still takes taps right away, so the cat can play.

Dynamic Type

Menu text grows up to 200% and screens scroll. The game's score and timer stop at 125%.

Reduce Motion

Calms the menus and intro, but not the cat's game.

The same tools we covered in class: labels, hints, traits, custom actions, announcements. The twist is deciding whose settings apply. An owner with Reduce Motion on still wants their cat to see critters move.

Building it

Why not just UIKit?

  • UIKit redraws when something changes: great for buttons, lists and forms.
  • A game changes every frame: up to 120 times a second, with critters moving, hiding and being caught.
  • So I kept UIKit for everything the owner touches, and used SpriteKit, Apple's built-in 2D game framework, for the playfield.
UIKit: score, timer, Home button
SpriteKit: critters, hiding spots, effects
UIKit: drawn background (pond, grass, snow)
The SpriteKit view is transparent and sits between two UIKit layers. Every other screen is plain UIKit with VIPER modules.
SpriteKit

If you know UIKit, you know SpriteKit

UIKitSpriteKitWhat it does
UIViewSKNodeSomething on screen, inside a tree of parents and children
UIImageViewSKSpriteNodeShows a picture, like a mouse or a beetle
UIView.animateSKAction"Move here over 1 second", without writing the frames yourself
touchesBegantouchesBeganThe same method, because nodes are UIKit responders too

SKView is a UIView, so the whole game sits inside a normal view controller.

One nice trick: a critter is nested nodes (path, facing, twitch, body), each running its own action, and SpriteKit adds them up. All critters sit under one "world" node, so pausing it freezes the round. For Unity folks: SKScene is a Scene, SKNode a GameObject with its Transform, SKSpriteNode a SpriteRenderer, update(_:) is Update(), and SKAction is like a tween.
Takeaways

What I learned

  • Start with who is holding the device, screen by screen.
  • When a user can't read, the design has to show, not tell.
  • Lay out for the space you have, not the device name.
  • UIKit for the app, SpriteKit for the action, and they fit together.
